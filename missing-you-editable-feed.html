Missing You Is a Part of Moving On

A personal geography of Shanghai. The photographs are the work: the image, erasure, handwriting, and legend belong together.

Rachael Tong

12 posts2007–2026Shanghai

Shanghai is where I was born, raised and am still living. These are photos of me from early childhood to young adulthood, with some of the people digitally erased in Procreate: by altering photographs that were taken to preserve someone’s presence, I make their absence visible.

Remembering isn’t the same as returning, and moving on doesn’t mean forgetting. Inspired by Geloy Concepcion’s erasure-based photography and Candace Chen’s NY Ghost in Severance.

Rachael TongThe old family house, Shanghai · 2008
p. 2

“We grew up before we realized it.”

Rachael TongShot in 2008, at the old house of my family celebrating my cousin’s 12th birthday. I used Procreate to digitally erase his face and write “We grew up before we realized it”.

The candles say twelve, but without his face my cousin becomes a figure of time rather than a single person. Growing up is only noticed in retrospect, once the photograph has become the one place where that afternoon still exists.

Rachael TongPlayground beside my uncle’s old house · 2007
p. 3

“I remember us before I learned to remember.”

Rachael TongShot in 2007, at the playground beside my uncle’s old house with my cousin when she came to Shanghai. I used Procreate to digitally erase her face and write “I remember us before I learned to remember”.

The photograph holds a memory from before memory became deliberate: a childhood relationship preserved, but no longer fully accessible. The two of us are still holding hands — the erasure keeps the gesture of closeness and removes only the detail that would make the memory exact.

Rachael TongThe old family house, Shanghai · 2007
p. 4

“Your love is real, so is your abuse.”

Rachael TongShot in 2007, at the old house of my family with my father. I used Procreate to digitally erase his face and write “your love is real, so is your abuse”.

A photograph cannot summarize an entire relationship. Affection and harm can coexist in the same history. I may remember someone’s kindness more easily than their flaws; the words written over his face refuse that idealized version and keep both truths in the frame.

Rachael TongThe old family house, Shanghai · 2007
p. 5

“Missing you is a part of moving on.”

Rachael TongShot in 2007, at the old house of my family. I used Procreate to digitally erase her face and write “missing you is a part of moving on”.

A single photograph cannot summarize an entire relationship, so I find myself patching up and reconstructing the past from these pieces. As I grow older, an unremarkable afternoon becomes a reminder of a period I can’t revisit. Pictures don’t carry fixed feelings; they trigger my experiences, and I read them differently as time passes.

Rachael TongGraduation party, Shanghai · 2023
p. 7

Scattered like stars ✶

Rachael TongShot in 2023 at a party with my friends after our senior high school graduation. I used Procreate to digitally erase their hands & arms and drew a star on each of them, because we literally scattered all over the world like stars in the sky.

The shared hotpot is still at the centre; only the hands reaching into it are gone, each pointing in a different direction. A star is distant but still visible — the friendship is no longer at one table, yet it hasn’t disappeared from the sky.

Rachael TongWindow shopping, Shanghai · 2021
p. 8

“Best ‘counselor’ ever.”

Rachael TongShot in 2021 with my friend X during window shopping. I used Procreate to digitally erase her body and wrote “best ‘counselor’ ever”.

Sometimes what we miss is not only a person, but also the version of ourselves who existed alongside them. The white silhouette keeps her exact outline in the mirror — the shape of someone who listened, still standing next to me.

Rachael TongDinner with friends, Shanghai · 2026
p. 9

“Maybe I miss you, maybe I miss who I was with you.”

Rachael TongShot in 2026 with my friend N and her boyfriend. I used Procreate to digitally erase their hands and wrote “maybe I miss you, maybe I miss who I was with you”.

The absence invites a question: am I remembering the person, or the self I was with them? Even the most recent photo in the series is already becoming a memory — nostalgia doesn’t wait for years to pass.

Rachael TongA small street in Shanghai · 2023
p. 10

“We used to be us. We are no longer us. We won’t be us any more.”

Rachael TongShot in 2023 when I caught a beetle in a small street in Shanghai with my friend K. I digitally erased their hand and wrote “we used to be us. We are no longer us. We won’t be us any more”.

Relationships change even when a photograph keeps one moment intact. The sentence moves through past, present and future, while our two shadows stay on the pavement — a trace the camera recorded that the erasure cannot fully remove.

Rachael TongPlaying Cyberpunk 2077 · 2025
p. 11

“Somewhere in my memory, we are still playing.”

Rachael TongShot in 2025 with my friend J when we were playing Cyberpunk 2077. I used Procreate to digitally erase her hand and wrote “Somewhere in my memory, we are still playing”.

In Severance, Ashley’s nostalgia draws her back to the past until she is imprisoned in revisiting it; Candace photographs the changing world and learns to consider what has disappeared without restoring it. My project sits somewhere between the two: I revisit the past while questioning the version of it that I have chosen to preserve.

Rachael TongMy dojo, Shanghai · 2024
p. 12

“Oss!”

Rachael TongShot in 2024 in my dojo in memory of my first two stripes in Brazilian jiu-jitsu — my friends were doing odd postures and chokeholds to me as a celebration. I digitally erased their faces and wrote “oss!”, an etiquette expression in many martial arts representing respect, gratitude and understanding.

Absence here does not erase the shared celebration; the gesture remains. Only the faces are gone — the gis, belts and playful chokeholds stay, because in this memory the body and the ritual matter more than who stood where.

Rachael TongSundial, Century Avenue · 2007 & 2026
p. 13

“Something old, something new.”

Rachael TongLeft: shot in 2007 at the sundial tourist attraction on Century Avenue with my cousin. I used Procreate to digitally erase his face and wrote “something old, something new”. Right: shot on October 17, 2026, at the same attraction from a different perspective.

The place remains recognisable, but the people and the person looking at it have changed. Side by side: in 2007 the sundial is a background to a family outing; in 2026 it is the subject, photographed alone, by the child who used to sit in the stroller.

Rachael TongMy high school volleyball court · 2024
p. 13

欲买桂花同载酒，终不似，少年游

Rachael TongShot in 2024 when I went back to my high school to visit my teachers — the volleyball court, since I always played volleyball with classmates back then. I used Procreate to digitally erase the students playing volleyball and wrote “欲买桂花同载酒，终不似，少年游” (I’d buy osmanthus and wine for old-time cheer — yet never taste that carefree spirit of my youth).

Moving on doesn’t always involve losing a person or leaving a place behind. Sometimes a familiar place continues to exist, but I no longer occupy the same role within it. I can return, recognise its meaning, and accept that I am no longer part of the scene in the same way.

Perhaps the Shanghai I remember never existed exactly as I remember it. It has been reconstructed through photos, relationships, and the person I have become. The people and places that shaped me remain part of my history, even when they no longer occupy the same place in my life.

The city remained. We changed.
And “missing you” became a part of moving on.
